# Ops jobs

Playground's background work runs as ops jobs: the Dashboard's supervisor, the sweeps, the merge train, the usage reads and the rest.
Each job is a setting, `jobs.<name>` (`ops-merge-train` is `jobs.opsMergeTrain`), whose value is the job: whether it is on, its schedule, its command or launchd program, and why it is paused.
A stored value holds only the fields it changes, and reads over the job's entry in Playground's registry, so a job turned on still runs the command and schedule Playground ships, including after an update changes them.
A fresh install has every job off but two: `jobs.opsDashboard`, the Dashboard's own, which serves first run's checks and every settings write, and `jobs.opsUsage`, which reads your Claude login's 5-hour and week usage every 5 minutes, so the Dashboard shows them with no setting.

## Turn a job on

Settings > Jobs on the Dashboard lists every job with what it does, when it runs, whether it is on and whether your settings set it.
Its switch saves the job's `enabled` field alone at your login's scope, with a reason, over any other field your scope's value holds.

A session cannot write a job's setting (the `no-extensions-write` hook refuses `playground settings set` and `reset` of every `jobs.<name>`, and `settings import-jobs` but its `--dry-run`), so it asks you with an Inbox guard decision: for a registry job its value is `{"enabled": true}` or `{"enabled": false}` alone, and for an org's own job it is the whole row ([below](#an-orgs-own-job)); your Approve writes it the same way ([Inbox](/docs/inbox)).
The one exception is how often a job runs: the Ops lead may set a job's row whose only change from the row your scope stores is `schedule.every`, its interval, where the job already runs on one and no other scope stores a row for it.
It does so with the `playground settings set` call alone on its line, since a variable set beside it could move the write to another scope.

From your own terminal, set your scope's value, which replaces any value it held, with a reason:

```bash
playground settings set jobs.opsSweep '{"enabled": true}' --reason "<why>" [--scope <scope>]
```

Your login's value wins over your org's, as for every setting, so a job you turn on or off holds whatever your org set; a field your value leaves out reads Playground's, not your org's.
Moving a schedule or pausing a job with `pausedFor` is the same kind of write, naming that field.

## How a change reaches the runner

At the top of every pass, the ops runner (`scripts/ops/up.py`) renders the jobs from your extensions into `<data>/ops/jobs.json` and writes a plist for each launchd job that is on into `<data>/ops/launch-agents`.
A machine with no extensions keeps its own `jobs.json`.
A plist whose job is now off moves to the trash under the `jobs` carve-out ([CLI](/docs/cli)); one the trash refuses stays, and the runner writes why to `<data>/ops/up.log`.
When that render cannot be trusted, the runner keeps the last `jobs.json` and writes why to `<data>/ops/up.log`.
A job the registry does not know, such as one of your own scripts, stays in `jobs.json` as it is, unless an org's own job of its name ([below](#an-orgs-own-job)) replaces it.
Each `script` item an org holds as a folder is installed into `<data>/ops/<key>/` in the same pass, beside the state its job keeps there, which the render never touches.

launchd reads `~/Library/LaunchAgents`, not `<data>/ops/launch-agents`: at the switch to settings, each rendered plist is copied into `~/Library/LaunchAgents` and loaded with one `launchctl bootstrap` per plist, by its own label, in place of the plists kept by hand.

## Bring an existing jobs.json in

A machine that ran its jobs from a hand-kept `jobs.json` brings them into settings once, at the switch:

```bash
playground settings import-jobs <jobs.json> [--launch-agents <dir>] [--scope <scope>] [--dry-run]
```

Each job the registry knows becomes its `jobs.<name>` setting at your login's scope (or `--scope`), on unless `jobs.json` disabled it, with a launchd job's program and folder read from its plist in `--launch-agents` (`~/Library/LaunchAgents` by default).
`--dry-run` prints what it would set and writes nothing.
You run it yourself: a session's run is refused but for `--dry-run`, since it would turn jobs on.
Every other job is listed as kept, and stays in `jobs.json`.

## Bring your own scripts in

A script your jobs run from a folder of its own (a runner, its helpers and the config it reads) becomes a `script` of your org once, at the switch:

```bash
playground ext import-scripts <ops-folder> <job>... --org <org> [--dry-run]
```

Each folder named becomes `<org>/scripts/<job>/` in your extensions: its top-level `.py`, `.sh` and `.json` files, but `state.json` and `watch.json`.
The run prints everything it leaves in the folder by name, so a dry run shows what stays behind.
A change to the script or its config is then a new revision, put with `playground ext put`, and the next pass installs it.

## An org's own job

An org runs a job of its own, one the registry does not know, by shipping its script and its row together in its extensions:

- the script as `<org>/scripts/<key>/`, which each pass installs into `<data>/ops/<key>/`;
- the row as `jobs.<camelName>` in `<org>/settings.json`, with every field a job has: `callsModel`, `command`, `description`, `enabled`, `heartbeat` and `schedule`, and `cwd` and `config` when it needs them.

You place the row, turn the job on or off and change its `config` with `playground settings set jobs.<camelName> '<row>' --scope <org> --reason "<why>"`, or by approving a guard decision that carries the row, and take it out with `playground settings reset jobs.<camelName> --scope <org>`.
The write checks the row as the render reads it (below), against the script folders that org's extensions hold at the time, and refuses it at your login's scope or when another org already adds the job.

```json
{
  "jobs.opsWatch": {
    "callsModel": false,
    "command": "{bun} --no-env-file {data}/ops/ops-watch/cli.ts --data {data}",
    "config": { "prefixes": ["INC"] },
    "description": "Watches the org's own queue.",
    "enabled": true,
    "heartbeat": "ops-watch",
    "schedule": { "always": true }
  }
}
```

The render takes the row only when it runs in a terminal, stays in its org's own script folder and keeps to its own name:

- its `command` is `{bun}`, then `--no-env-file` if it wants it, then a script in a folder that org ships, `{data}/ops/<key>/<file>`, then plain words or `{data}`;
- its `cwd` is that folder or one under it, and that folder when the row leaves it out;
- its `heartbeat` is the job's own name, and it names no `stop` group, since ops stops a job's group before each start.

Any other row is refused: one off that shape, one naming `launchd` or `program`, a second org's row for a job the first adds (scopes in A to Z order), and a row for such a job at your login's scope.
Each refusal is a `jobs: refused <scope> <key>: <why>` line in `<data>/ops/up.log`, and its job does not run; the rest of the render is written as usual.
The job's `jobs.json` row names its org in `org`, so when its row is later refused or removed, the job stays listed as paused and the runner stops it, until a row of its name renders again.
A pass that cannot read the orgs at all keeps each org's job as the last `jobs.json` had it, and says so in `up.log`.

`config` is the job's own settings.
Playground checks only that it is an object, and writes it into the job's `jobs.json` row, where the job reads it and checks each field itself.
A registry job's row takes a `config` the same way.
